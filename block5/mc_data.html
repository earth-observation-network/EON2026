Sensors

The sensors used in the EON microclimate work are produced by Fine Offset and re-branded or partly modified by different resellers. This article focuses on products sold under the German froggit brand and the international Ecowitt product family. More precisely, it covers the DP-/GW SmartHub family of Wi-Fi gateways, the mobile WS6210S field gateway, and compatible radio-connected sensors.

All components used in this tutorial must be the European 868 MHz versions.

Manufacturer, brands, and model names

Shenzhen Fine Offset Electronics develops and manufactures the underlying hardware and radio ecosystem. Ecowitt is the manufacturer’s international retail brand. Froggit and several other regional suppliers sell Fine Offset hardware under their own product names and may combine it with different enclosures, firmware, documentation, cloud services, or accessories.

These companies are therefore described here as resellers or re-branders. The term licensee should only be used where a corresponding contractual relationship is documented.

The same or closely related hardware can consequently appear under several names:

WH31 / WN31 DP50 air temperature and relative humidity
WN35 DP10 leaf wetness
WH51 DP100 soil moisture
WH41 / WH43 DP200 particulate matter
WH45 DP250 indoor air quality
WH57 DP60 lightning detection
WS6210S WittField WS6210S solar-powered mobile 4G/Wi-Fi field gateway

Other brands, including Ambient Weather, Sainlogic, Misol, Watson, and various retailer labels, also sell products based on Fine Offset designs. However, visual similarity or a related model name does not guarantee interoperability. A reseller may alter firmware, supported services, channel limits, or radio configuration, and not every product sold by these brands necessarily originates from Fine Offset.

For compatibility checks, use the underlying Fine Offset/Ecowitt model identifier whenever it is available and verify all of the following:

  • radio frequency - 868 MHz for this European network;
  • sensor type and radio protocol;
  • gateway firmware and supported channel count;
  • cloud, local API, and application support;
  • regional hardware version rather than housing appearance alone.

The practical strength of the SmartHub ecosystem is the large range of compatible radio-connected sensors. The exact number of simultaneously supported sensors depends on the gateway, its firmware, and the sensor type. The linked manuals are authoritative for operating instructions and technical specifications.

Compatible sensors

Up to 8 channels

  • DP150 / WN34S soil-temperature sensor - manual and specifications
  • DP50 / WH31 / WN31 multi-channel temperature and relative-humidity sensor - manual and specifications
  • WN34L wire-probe liquid-temperature sensor - manual and specifications
  • DP10 / WN35 leaf-wetness sensor - manual and specifications
  • DP100 / WH51 soil-moisture sensor - manual and specifications

Current gateways and firmware may support up to 16 WH51 soil-moisture channels. Older gateways may expose only eight channels. The gateway manual and installed firmware are therefore authoritative for the actual channel limit.

Up to 4 channels

  • DP200 / WH41 / WH43 PM2.5/PM10 sensor - manual and specifications
  • WH55 water-leak sensor - manual and specifications

Up to 2 channels

  • DP250 / WH45 indoor CO2, PM2.5, PM10, temperature, and relative-humidity sensor - manual and specifications
  • DP60 / WH57 lightning sensor - manual and specifications

The WH55 is the water-leak sensor. The WH45 is the indoor five-in-one air-quality sensor.

Six-in-one outdoor sensor

  • WS80 ultrasonic six-in-one outdoor sensor - manual and specifications

The WS80 measures:

  • wind speed;
  • wind direction;
  • air temperature;
  • relative humidity;
  • UV index;
  • solar radiation.

The WS80 does not measure precipitation. Rainfall requires a separate compatible 868 MHz rain gauge such as the WH40.

Older integrated arrays such as the WS69/WH65 combine wind, rain, radiation, temperature, and humidity in one unit but use a different sensor design.

This tutorial requires the European 868 MHz version of the WS80 and all additional radio sensors.

Mobile field uplink gateway

  • WS6210S manual - 4G and Wi-Fi mobile gateway with external RF antenna
  • WS6210/WS6210S product page - configurations and accessories

The WS6210S, rather than the GW1200, is the mobile uplink gateway intended for the autonomous field installation described here. It receives measurements from compatible radio sensors and uploads them using either 4G/LTE or Wi-Fi. It can therefore be operated at field sites without permanent access to a local network.

The WS6210S includes:

  • an external sub-GHz RF antenna;
  • a solar panel;
  • rechargeable batteries;
  • a SIM-card facility;
  • local microSD storage.

The WS6210S is a gateway rather than a sensor. Weather and soil measurements are supplied by separately connected devices such as the WS80, WN34S, WH31, or WH51.

The sensor radio connection and the internet uplink use different frequencies and perform different tasks:

  • 868 MHz connects the sensors to the WS6210S;
  • 4G/LTE or Wi-Fi transfers the collected measurements from the gateway to the server.

The gateway and every connected radio sensor must use mutually compatible European 868 MHz hardware versions.

Calibration and quality-control concept

Low-cost sensors should not be assumed to provide stable and directly comparable measurements without prior checks. Reliable microclimate data therefore require a calibration and quality-control concept before the measurements are used for temporal analysis or spatial modelling.

1. Sensor intercomparison

All sensors of the same type should first be operated together under conditions that are as similar as possible. A controlled environment such as a climate chamber is preferable.

The simultaneous measurements can be used to identify:

  • systematic differences between sensors;
  • individual sensors with conspicuous offsets;
  • differences in response time;
  • implausible values or intermittent transmission failures;
  • sensors with unusually high variability.

This intercomparison identifies relative differences within the sensor network. By itself, it does not establish traceability to an external reference.

2. Comparison with a reference station

The sensors should subsequently be compared with an operational, higher-quality reference station under field conditions.

This comparison should cover environmental conditions relevant to the intended deployment, including different temperature and humidity levels where possible. It provides evidence about systematic deviations from the reference and about whether corrections derived under controlled conditions remain applicable in the field.

3. Documentation and correction

The following information should be retained for every sensor:

  • manufacturer and reseller designation;
  • underlying Fine Offset/Ecowitt model identifier;
  • individual sensor ID;
  • radio channel;
  • regional frequency version;
  • gateway and firmware version;
  • installation and radiation-shield configuration;
  • dates and conditions of the intercomparison;
  • reference instrument;
  • detected offset or correction model;
  • sensors excluded from subsequent analysis.

Any correction must be applied to the individual sensor rather than indiscriminately to the entire product type.

Calibration and intercomparison do not remove all measurement uncertainty. They document whether the sensors are sufficiently consistent for the intended temporal and spatial comparison.
